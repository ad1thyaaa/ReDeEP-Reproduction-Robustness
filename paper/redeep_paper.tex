\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is meant to ground a language model in retrieved evidence, yet models still hallucinate even when the evidence is correct. ReDeEP (Sun et al., ICLR 2025) detects such hallucinations by reading a model's internals: it measures how much \emph{copying heads} use the retrieved context and how much \emph{knowledge FFNs} inject parametric memory. We reproduce ReDeEP on the RAGTruth benchmark with a single 6\,GB laptop GPU. On the paper's own backbone, Llama-2-7B-chat, our detector reaches an AUC of 0.739 against the published 0.7325, and the paper's two mechanistic findings replicate: all 1024 heads show lower context usage in hallucinated responses, and later-layer FFNs inject more parametric knowledge. Across four LLaMA-family models from 1.1B to 7B parameters, ReDeEP outperforms perplexity and entropy baselines by 0.06 to 0.11 AUC, although per-task AUCs are markedly lower than the pooled figure. We then stress-test the method under corrupted retrieval. An irrelevant passage raises the hallucination score sharply, but corrupting the passage's numbers, so that the response contradicts it, leaves the score unchanged or lowers it: ReDeEP measures whether a response is \emph{about} its context, not whether it \emph{agrees} with it. Finally, prompted black-box judges from the Gemini and GPT families (AUC 0.827 and 0.790) outperform ReDeEP (0.721) on the same held-out responses, while a small 3B LLaMA judge performs at chance (0.499). Using the authors' released code and data, we also reproduce chunk-level ReDeEP (leakage-free AUC 0.731, identical to the authors' own logged scores), evaluate on Dolly (AC), and audit the official evaluation: selecting heads and layers on the test set inflates AUC by 0.016 on RAGTruth and 0.081 on Dolly (AC). The AARF mitigation's gate fires on every decoding step with the released hyperparameters, and its apparent win over the baseline largely disappears once the judge's position bias is controlled.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large language models (LLMs) answer fluently but are prone to \emph{hallucination}: generating content that is not supported by any evidence. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the standard remedy. A retriever fetches passages relevant to the user's query, and the model is instructed to answer from them. RAG is therefore widely deployed where trust matters, such as legal research, medical question answering and enterprise search.

RAG does not remove the problem. Even when the retrieved passage is accurate and contains the answer, models still produce claims that contradict or go beyond it. A model may be given a passage stating that a lunar mission returned 1,935.3\,g of samples and still answer with a different number recalled from its training data. If a model can hallucinate when the correct answer is in front of it, adding retrieval is not a sufficient fix. We also need detectors that tell when a response has drifted away from its context.

ReDeEP~\cite{sun2025redeep} approaches this through \emph{mechanistic interpretability}: rather than judging the output text, it inspects how the model produced it. Its central hypothesis is that two internal components compete. \emph{Copying heads}, attention heads that move information from context tokens into the residual stream, carry the external knowledge. \emph{Knowledge FFNs}, feed-forward layers in the later part of the network, inject the model's stored, parametric knowledge. A RAG hallucination occurs when the model under-uses the first and over-relies on the second. ReDeEP turns both effects into scores and combines them into a hallucination detector.

Our review of the method, prepared for this project, identified four gaps: it is validated only on LLaMA-family models, its head and layer selection is tuned by hand, its mitigation (AARF) gives only modest gains, and it \emph{assumes the retrieved context is accurate}, never testing noisy or wrong retrieval, a common failure in deployed systems. This project reproduces the method and then targets the last gap directly.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproduction of ReDeEP's token-level detector on RAGTruth~\cite{niu2024ragtruth} on consumer hardware (RTX~4050, 6\,GB VRAM). Memory optimisations make 7B models feasible, and the result matches the published AUC.
\item A replication of the paper's two mechanistic findings, and a transfer study across four LLaMA-family backbones~\cite{touvron2023llama2} from 1.1B to 7B parameters, with ROC, per-task and ablation analyses.
\item A robustness extension that holds each response fixed and corrupts the retrieved passage, showing that ReDeEP detects off-topic context but not contradicted facts.
\item A comparison against prompted black-box judges from the GPT, Gemini and LLaMA families on the same held-out responses.
\item Using the authors' released code and data: chunk-level ReDeEP, the Dolly (AC) dataset, an audit of the official evaluation protocol, and an evaluation of the AARF mitigation with position-bias control.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{RAG Hallucination versus Knowledge Conflict}
ReDeEP distinguishes two failure modes. A \emph{knowledge conflict} arises when the retrieved context itself is wrong or contradicts what the model knows. A \emph{RAG hallucination} arises when the context is accurate and relevant, yet the response contradicts it or adds unsupported content. The paper targets only the second. Its datasets are chosen so that the context can be trusted, and the response is the only thing that can be wrong. Our robustness extension (Section~\ref{sec:robust}) deliberately crosses this boundary to see how the method behaves just outside its stated scope.

\subsection{Families of Hallucination Detectors}
Sun et al.\ group prior detectors into three families by which signal they read and what confounds it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Parametric confounded by external (PCE)} methods read hidden states to estimate what the model knows, for example EigenScore, SEP, SAPLMA and ITI. The external context mixes into these states and confounds the reading.
\item \emph{External confounded by parametric (ECP)} methods check the response against the context with prompts or retrieval-style checks, for example RAGAS, ChainPoll and RefChecker. The checking model's own knowledge leaks into the judgement.
\item \emph{Mixed parametric and external (MPE)} methods use uncertainty from sampling or token probabilities, for example perplexity, energy and LN-Entropy~\cite{malinin2021uncertainty}. They blend both knowledge sources without separating them.
\end{itemize}
None of these \emph{decouples} context usage from parametric-knowledge usage. ReDeEP's claim is that decoupling them gives a cleaner signal. In our experiments, perplexity and LN-Entropy represent the MPE family, and prompted LLM judges represent the ECP family.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability Tools}
Transformer language models maintain a \emph{residual stream}: each layer reads it, and its attention and FFN blocks add their outputs back into it. Two tools let ReDeEP read this stream. First, each attention head's \emph{OV circuit} ($W_V W_O$) describes what information the head writes when it attends to a token. A head whose OV circuit maps a token's embedding back towards the same token's unembedding is a \emph{copying head}. Second, the \emph{logit lens}~\cite{logitlens} applies the model's final norm and unembedding $W_U$ to an intermediate residual state, giving the next-token distribution the model ``would predict'' at that depth. Prior work shows that FFN layers act as key-value memories that store factual knowledge~\cite{geva2021ffn}, which motivates measuring what each FFN adds.

\section{The ReDeEP Method}

\caption{ReDeEP pipeline: one forward pass yields an external-context score per head and a parametric-knowledge score per FFN layer; a logistic regression combines them into a hallucination score.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the detector. Given a prompt (instruction plus retrieved passage) and a response, ReDeEP runs one forward pass over their concatenation. Let $R$ be the set of response token positions and $C$ the set of context positions.

\subsection{External Context Score (ECS)}
For head $h$ in layer $l$ and response token $n$, let $\mathcal{T}_n\subset C$ be the 10\% of context tokens that the head attends to most. With $x^{L}_i$ the final-layer hidden state of token $i$,
\begin{equation}
E^{l,h} = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{n\in R}\cos\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_n|}\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_n} x^{L}_{i},\; x^{L}_{n}\Big).
\end{equation}
ECS asks whether the content a head read is reflected in what the model wrote, not merely whether it attended to the right place. The paper finds that about 77.5\% of hallucinations occur even though the heads attend to the correct context, so attention location alone is not enough.

\subsection{Parametric Knowledge Score (PKS)}
For layer $l$, the residual state just before the FFN block ($x^{\mathrm{mid},l}$) and just after the whole layer ($x^{l}$) are decoded with the logit lens. With $p_n = \sigma(W_U\,\mathrm{norm}(x_n))$ the resulting next-token distribution,
\begin{equation}
P^{l} = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{n\in R}\mathrm{JSD}\big(p^{\mathrm{mid},l}_n,\; p^{l}_n\big),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the softmax and JSD the Jensen--Shannon divergence. A large divergence means the FFN changed what the model wanted to say, so it injected its own knowledge.

\subsection{Selecting Copying Heads and Knowledge FFNs}
Copying heads are found from weights alone. For each head we form the matrix $M = W_E\,W_V^{h}W_O^{h}\,W_U^{\top}$ restricted to a fixed random sample of 500 vocabulary tokens. A copying head has a large-magnitude trace, meaning tokens map strongly onto themselves, and few Gershgorin-disc outliers, meaning a well-conditioned diagonal. Heads are ranked by the sum of both ranks, and the top $|\mathcal{A}|=8$ form the set $\mathcal{A}$. Knowledge FFNs are chosen with labels. On each training fold, the $|\mathcal{F}|=5$ layers whose PKS correlates most positively with the hallucination label form the set $\mathcal{F}$.

\subsection{Detector}
ReDeEP models the hallucination score as $H = \alpha\,\bar{P}_{\mathcal{F}} - \beta\,\bar{E}_{\mathcal{A}}$, with $\alpha,\beta>0$, where the bars denote means over the selected layers and heads. We fit this as a logistic regression on the two standardised features $\bar{P}_{\mathcal{F}}$ and $-\bar{E}_{\mathcal{A}}$. Its probability output is the score, and the decision threshold is the best-F1 point on the training fold. Algorithm~\ref{alg:redeep} gives the scoring procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ReDeEP scoring of one response}
\label{alg:redeep}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require prompt $c$, response $r$, heads $\mathcal{A}$, layers $\mathcal{F}$, fitted regression $g$
\State run one forward pass on $[c; r]$; hooks record per layer the top-$k$ attended context indices and $x^{\mathrm{mid},l}$
\For{each head $(l,h)\in\mathcal{A}$}
  \State $E^{l,h}\gets$ mean over $n\in R$ of $\cos(\text{mean}_{i\in\mathcal{T}_n} x^{L}_i,\, x^{L}_n)$
\EndFor
\For{each layer $l\in\mathcal{F}$}
  \State $P^{l}\gets$ mean over $n\in R$ of $\mathrm{JSD}(p^{\mathrm{mid},l}_n, p^{l}_n)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $g\big(\mathrm{mean}_{\mathcal{F}}P,\; -\mathrm{mean}_{\mathcal{A}}E\big)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{AARF Mitigation}
The paper also proposes AARF (``Add Attention, Reduce FFN''). At decoding time it scales copying-head outputs by $\alpha_2>1$ and knowledge-FFN outputs by $\beta_2<1$, steering generation towards the context without fine-tuning. In the released code the intervention is gated per token: it is applied only when the token-level ReDeEP score, min-max normalised with stored ranges, exceeds a threshold (0.6, with $\alpha_2=1.2$, $\beta_2=0.8$). We port this gate faithfully and evaluate AARF in Section~\ref{sec:aarf}.

\subsection{Chunk-Level ReDeEP}
ReDeEP(chunk) works on text chunks instead of single tokens. Prompt and response are split into chunks (the released \texttt{prompt\_spans} and \texttt{response\_spans}). For each response chunk and copying head, ECS is the BGE~\cite{bge} embedding similarity between the response chunk and the prompt chunk that receives the most attention mass from it under that head. PKS is the token-summed JSD of each layer over the chunk. The authors use their own top-32 copying heads per model, which they release; we use the same heads. Our hooks keep only these heads' chunk-level attention sums, so long prompts fit in 6\,GB.

\section{Dataset}
\subsection{RAGTruth}
RAGTruth~\cite{niu2024ragtruth} is the first large word-level hallucination corpus built specifically for RAG. It contains 17,790 responses to 2,965 source prompts. Each response was generated by one of six LLMs: GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, Llama-2-7B/13B/70B-chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct. Human annotators marked every hallucinated span with its character offsets and type. It covers three task types: question answering over MS MARCO passages, summarisation of news articles, and data-to-text generation from structured Yelp business records.

The corpus has two files. \texttt{source\_info.jsonl} holds each source's identifier, task type and full prompt including the retrieved context. \texttt{response.jsonl} holds each response with its generator model, train/test split, a quality flag for refusals or truncation, and the list of hallucination spans. Following the paper, a response is labelled hallucinated if it contains at least one span. We keep responses flagged as good quality.

\textbf{Evaluation subset.} The paper evaluates each backbone on responses that backbone generated. We use the Llama-2-7B-chat setting: all good-quality \emph{test-split} responses generated by llama-2-7b-chat, capped at 6{,}000 characters of prompt plus response so that every example fits in 1{,}024 tokens. This yields 415 responses, of which 212 (51\%) are hallucinated. Table~\ref{tab:data} breaks them down by task.

\subsection{Dolly (AC)}
The paper also evaluates on Dolly (AC), a subset of Databricks' dolly-15k instruction data filtered to prompts with an accurate context passage, answered by each backbone and hand-labelled. The authors' released dataset contains it: 100 question-answering prompts with responses from Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B and Llama-3-8B (99 test responses each). Dolly (AC) carries only a response-level label (hallucinated or not), with no span positions, so we evaluate it at the response level. The release also includes Llama-3-8B-Instruct's own RAGTruth responses (450 test responses) and the chunk boundaries used by chunk-level ReDeEP.

\subsection{Hardware and Software}
All experiments ran on a Lenovo LOQ laptop with an NVIDIA RTX~4050 Laptop GPU (6\,GB VRAM) and 16\,GB RAM, under Windows~11. We used PyTorch~2.11 with CUDA~12.8, Hugging Face transformers~5.17 and bitsandbytes~0.50. The 3B and 7B models were loaded in 4-bit NF4 with fp16 computation; the smaller models ran in fp16.

\subsection{Engineering for a 6\,GB GPU}
The reference pipeline asks the model to return every full attention matrix. For Llama-2-7B at 1{,}024 tokens that is 32 layers $\times$ 32 heads $\times$ $1024^2$ values, which does not fit beside the weights. We made three changes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Forward hooks on each attention module keep only the top-$k$ context indices per head and response token, then discard the attention map.
\item The mean of attended hidden states is computed as a sparse averaging-matrix product instead of gathering a $[H, |R|, k, d]$ tensor.
\item Perplexity and LN-Entropy reuse the logits of the same forward pass instead of a second pass.
\end{enumerate}
On a RAGTruth example, the rewritten ECS matched the original implementation to within $6\times10^{-8}$, so results remain comparable. Copying-head weights are dequantised from 4-bit on the fly. Prompts longer than the budget are truncated from the left, so the whole response is always scored.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
We report AUC (threshold-free ranking quality), the Pearson correlation (PCC) between score and label, and recall and F1 at the training-fold threshold. Our main protocol is stratified 5-fold cross-validation over the 415 responses, reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over folds. Copying heads are fixed from weights, and knowledge FFNs and the regression are re-fitted inside each training fold, so no test label influences selection. For comparisons with the judges we also use a single fixed 70/30 split, whose test part has 124 responses.

\subsection{Baselines}
\textbf{Perplexity} is the exponentiated mean negative log-likelihood of the response tokens given the prompt. \textbf{LN-Entropy} is the mean per-token entropy of the predictive distribution over the response~\cite{malinin2021uncertainty}. Both come from the same forward pass and backbone as ReDeEP. Higher values are taken to indicate hallucination.

\subsection{Black-Box Judges}
Each judge receives the RAG input and the response and replies in JSON with the probability (0--100) that the response contains a hallucination (prompt in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). We used Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite through the Google AI Studio free tier. For the GPT family we used OpenAI's open-weights gpt-oss-120b, served free by Groq. We also ran Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct locally in 4-bit. The stated probability is the score.

The paper reports two core observations on Llama-2-7B: copying heads use less context in hallucinated responses (1006 of 1024 heads), and later-layer FFNs inject more parametric knowledge. Both replicate (Table~\ref{tab:mech}). On Llama-2-7B, all 1024 of 1024 heads have higher mean ECS on truthful responses (Fig.~\ref{fig:ecsmap}). One caution applies to the ECS result. Because ECS compares final-layer hidden states for every head, the per-head scores of a response are strongly correlated. The near-unanimous head count therefore reflects a shift at the level of the whole response more than 1024 independent effects.

On Llama-2-7B it is 0.623 on QA, 0.561 on summarisation and 0.396 on data-to-text. ReDeEP beats the baselines on QA for every backbone, but the baselines win on summarisation for three of the four models, and on data-to-text ReDeEP is near or below chance for the three Llama models.

Part of the pooled score therefore comes from separating \emph{tasks}, not responses. The hallucination rate differs strongly by task in our subset: 35\% for QA, 35\% for summarisation and 81\% for data-to-text. Internal signals also differ systematically by task. Data-to-text prompts are structured JSON, whose hidden states plausibly look unlike prose, so a detector may partly identify the task and inherit its base rate. The paper reports pooled numbers too, so the comparison with it is fair, but within-task evaluation is the more demanding test for deployment.

\section{Extension: Robustness Under Corrupted Retrieval}
\label{sec:robust}
ReDeEP assumes accurate context. We ask what its signals measure when that assumption is broken on purpose. Two outcomes are possible. If ECS and PKS track \emph{faithfulness to the given context}, a response should look hallucinated whenever its context stops supporting it. If they track something weaker, such as topical similarity, some kinds of contradiction will go unnoticed.

\subsection{Design}
We hold each response fixed and change only the retrieved passage, under three conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Accurate:} the real RAGTruth passage (control).
\item \emph{Irrelevant:} a passage from a different source of the same task type. Nothing in the response is supported any more.
\item \emph{Numbers corrupted:} every number in the real passage is multiplied by a random factor in $[1.3, 3.0]$, with a fixed seed per example. Numbers the response copied are now contradicted by the passage, while the topic and wording are unchanged.
\end{enumerate}
We draw 150 responses (75 hallucinated), refit the detector on the remaining 265, and score each response under all three passages with TinyLlama-1.1B and Llama-2-7B. A faithful detector should raise its score under both corrupted conditions. We test the per-response score change with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Irrelevant passages work as intended.} The score jumps for nearly every response, by +0.317 for TinyLlama and +0.250 for Llama-2-7B (both $p<0.001$). The original labels then stop separating the responses (AUC $\approx$0.55; Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, Table~\ref{tab:robust}). This is correct, because nothing in a response is supported by the new passage. The mechanism differs by size. In TinyLlama, ECS falls from 0.326 to 0.244, as copying heads find nothing useful to copy. In Llama-2-7B, ECS is flat and the increase comes from PKS (0.091 to 0.114): the larger model falls back on its own knowledge.

\textbf{Corrupted numbers go unnoticed.} For TinyLlama the score does not move (+0.005, $p=0.40$). For Llama-2-7B it \emph{falls} slightly (-0.010, $p<0.001$) while ECS rises from 0.282 to 0.310, so the detector rates a contradicted response as more faithful. The corruption may not touch every response, so we checked the 122 responses that state a number the corrupted passage now contradicts. Their score rose in only 52\% of cases for TinyLlama, which is chance, and 31\% for Llama-2-7B. Detection AUC against the original labels is also unchanged under this condition, so the detector behaves as if the passage were still accurate.

\subsection{Interpretation}
ECS is a cosine similarity between hidden states. It measures whether the response is built from the same \emph{content} the copying heads read, not whether individual facts agree. A changed number in an otherwise identical sentence plausibly moves the hidden state very little, so ReDeEP cannot see it. PKS does not compensate: the FFNs inject no more parametric knowledge when the passage's numbers change.

ReDeEP is therefore a detector of \emph{topical} hallucination: a response that drifts away from what the context is about. It is not a detector of \emph{factual} inconsistency with the context. In deployment, it should be paired with a fact-level check, such as matching numbers and entities between response and context, or a text-level judge. Notably, the paper's motivating example is itself a wrong number, which is the kind of error our experiment suggests ReDeEP is least sensitive to.

\section{AARF Mitigation}
\label{sec:aarf}
\subsection{Setup}
Following the released scripts, we generated greedy answers to the 99 Dolly (AC) test prompts with Llama-2-7B, once normally and once with gated AARF, using the authors' Llama-2-7B Dolly hyperparameters (4 heads, 3 layers, weight 0.2, $\alpha_2=1.2$, $\beta_2=0.8$, threshold 0.6). The authors judged each differing pair with GPT-4o, always showing the AARF answer first and asking which is more truthful. We use the same prompt with Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (free tier). We judge every pair in \emph{both} orders, because LLM judges are known to favour one position.

\subsection{The Gate Always Fires}
We logged the gate score at every decoding step. Across all 10,161 steps, the normalised score never fell below 0.83 (median 1.26), so the 0.6 threshold was exceeded on 100\% of steps. The reason is a scale mismatch in the released configuration. The step score normalises a \emph{mean} over layers and heads with min-max ranges recorded for \emph{sums} at the response level, and the final range is very narrow ($[-0.100, -0.029]$). Typical values (PKS $\approx$2.5 against a range of 0--216; ECS $\approx$0.2) give a combined score near $-0.007$, far above the cut-off. In our faithful port, AARF therefore behaves as an always-on intervention rather than a targeted one.

\subsection{Results}

AARF changed the answer for 63 of 99 prompts; the other 36 were identical. Judged in the official order, AARF wins 33 pairs and the baseline 24 (6 verdicts unparsable), comparable to the modest gains the paper reports (Table~\ref{tab:aarf}). However, the judge reversed its verdict when the two answers were swapped in 22 of 63 pairs (35\%). Counting only pairs where both orders agree, AARF wins 19 and the baseline 15, a difference well within chance (two-sided sign test $p=0.61$). Much of AARF's apparent advantage in the official evaluation is therefore attributable to position bias. Its effect on truthfulness, in our setting, is not distinguishable from zero.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{What reproduces.} The headline detection result reproduces closely on a laptop GPU, despite 4-bit weights and a sampled vocabulary for copying-head analysis. So do both mechanistic observations. The method also transfers to smaller and newer LLaMA models with little loss, which is encouraging for low-cost use.

\textbf{What the pooled number hides.} Per-task AUCs are 0.08--0.35 lower than pooled AUCs, and on data-to-text ReDeEP is unreliable for three of the four backbones. Evaluating hallucination detectors within task, or reporting both numbers, would give a more realistic picture of deployment performance.

\textbf{Internals versus judges.} Reading internals is cheap, local and needs no prompt, but on our data it is less accurate than asking a strong model. The two approaches fail differently. ReDeEP misses contradicted facts, while the judges are coarse and over-flag. A combination, for example ReDeEP as a free first-pass filter and a judge on flagged responses, is a natural next step.

%=====================================================================
\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Quantisation:} the 3B and 7B models ran in 4-bit NF4, so their ECS and PKS approximate full precision. The close match with the paper suggests the effect is small.
\item \emph{Sampled copying-head scores:} OV circuits are scored on 500 sampled vocabulary tokens rather than the full vocabulary of 32k to 128k tokens.
\item \emph{One generator:} all responses come from llama-2-7b-chat, so the other three backbones score text they did not write. This differs from the paper's matched setting.
\item \emph{AARF judge:} AARF answers were judged by Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite instead of GPT-4o, on one backbone.
\item \emph{Dolly (AC) size:} 99 responses per backbone give fold standard deviations near 0.08--0.10 AUC.
\item \emph{Sample size:} 415 responses give fold standard deviations of 0.03--0.05 AUC, and differences below about 0.05 are within noise. Judge comparisons use 124 responses.
\item \emph{Robustness scope:} only numbers are corrupted. Entity swaps, negations and partially relevant passages are untested.
\item \emph{Judge access:} judges ran once, at default sampling settings, on free tiers. The GPT-family judge is the open-weights gpt-oss-120b, not a proprietary GPT-4-class model.
\end{itemize}

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We reproduced ReDeEP on a 6\,GB laptop GPU. The detector reaches an AUC of 0.739 on Llama-2-7B against 0.7325 in the paper, both of the paper's mechanistic findings replicate, and the method beats uncertainty baselines on four LLaMA models from 1.1B to 7B parameters. Its accuracy is insensitive to the number of selected heads and layers, and both of its signals contribute.

With the authors' released code and data, chunk-level ReDeEP reproduces exactly (leakage-free AUC 0.731, identical to their own logged scores), and the method also works on Dolly (AC). The official evaluation selects features on the test set; this inflates results slightly on RAGTruth (0.016) and substantially on the small Dolly (AC) set (0.081). The AARF mitigation's gate fires on every step with the released settings, and its benefit vanishes once the judge's position bias is controlled.

Our extension shows the method's boundary. ReDeEP catches responses that are off-topic for their context but not responses that contradict their context's numbers. Prompted judges from the Gemini and GPT families are more accurate, while small prompted judges do not work at all.

ReDeEP is therefore best used as a cheap, private first-line detector for topical hallucinations, paired with a fact-level check. Future work includes entity and negation corruptions, a numeric-consistency feature combined with ECS, full-precision runs on larger GPUs, a recalibrated AARF gate, and judge evaluation with repeated sampling.
